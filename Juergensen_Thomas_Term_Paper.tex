\section{Introduction}\label{sec-intro}

In many empirical applications, researchers employ multiple instrumental
variables in \textbf{two-stage
least squares} (\textbf{2SLS}) estimation in order to improve
statistical efficiency. When treatment effects are constant, combining
several valid instruments strengthens the first stage, reduces sampling
variance, and leads to more precise estimates. In such settings, the use
of multiple instruments is uncontroversial and often desirable, and
efficiency considerations alone provide a compelling justification for
their use.

The standard causal interpretation of 2SLS is rooted in the Local
Average Treatment Effect (LATE) framework of Imbens and Angrist (1994).
With a single binary instrument alongside a standard monotonicity
assumption, 2SLS identifies the average treatment effect for those
individuals whose treatment status is changed by the instrument, this
kind of individuals are known as the \textbf{compliers}. This
interpretation has become a cornerstone of modern empirical practice.
When multiple instruments are available, Angrist and Imbens (1995)
showed that the 2SLS estimand can be interpreted as a positively
weighted average of local average treatment effects (LATEs) for
subpopulations whose treatment status is affected by the instruments.

However, justifying this interpretation in the multiple-instrument
setting requires a strong generalization of the standard monotonicity
assumption. As shown by Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021), this
assumption is extremely restrictive and is frequently violated once
treatment effects are heterogeneous. Importantly, the problem does not
arise from high dimensionality or technical complexity. Instead, it is
inherent to the use of more than one instrument, even in the simplest
possible environments.

To make this point transparent, this paper focuses on the simplest
nontrivial setting: a binary treatment and two binary instrumental
variables. Even in this minimal environment, the standard monotonicity
assumption, which will be called ``Imbens and Angrist monotonicity'', or
\textbf{IAM}, imposes strong restrictions on individual treatment choice
behavior. With four possible instrument states, the assumption requires
that each individual's treatment decisions across these states can be
ordered in a single, consistent direction. As a result, individuals
cannot respond to different instruments in meaningfully different ways.

This restriction is difficult to reconcile with applied work. In
empirical practice, different instruments typically represent distinct
sources of exogenous variation, such as institutional rules, policy
thresholds, or geographic constraints. It is therefore natural to expect
that different instruments affect different individuals and move
different margins of the population into treatment. Nevertheless, such
instruments are often combined in a single 2SLS regression and
interpreted as if they were reinforcing the same causal effect. The key
insight of Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021) is that IAM does
much more than rule out defiers: in the presence of multiple
instruments, it effectively rules out economically meaningful
heterogeneity in how different instruments affect treatment.

The main contribution of the paper is to make this restriction explicit
and to propose a more credible alternative. The authors introduce
\textbf{partial
monotonicity}, which requires that each instrument, holding the other
instruments fixed, affects treatment in a weakly monotone way. This
weaker assumption allows different instruments to influence different
subpopulations, while still ruling out perverse responses to each
instrument individually.

Under partial monotonicity, 2SLS continues to admit a causal
interpretation, but its nature changes in an important way. Rather than
identifying a single local average treatment effect, the 2SLS estimand
becomes a weighted average of multiple group-specific causal effects,
and these weights do not need to be all positive. The paper provides a
complete characterization of this estimand, clarifies the economic
meaning of the resulting weights, and shows how their signs are directly
linked to observable first-stage relationships.

The remainder of this term paper proceeds as follows. Section 2 will
provide a detailed overview of Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters'
methodology and framework, introduces IAM and partial monotonicity
assumptions, and discusses their implications for treatment choice
behavior. Section 5 examines the empirical implications of the framework
and illustrates the results using a simulation. Section 6 discusses
limitations, open questions, and potential extensions, and concludes.

\subsection{Literature Review}\label{literature-review}

The starting point for the present paper is the classic LATE
interpretation of IV under heterogeneity. Imbens and Angrist (1994)
established that with a single binary instrument and monotonicity, IV
identifies a complier average causal effect. Angrist and Imbens (1995)
then extended this logic to multiple instruments, showing that 2SLS can
be interpreted as a positively weighted average of instrument-specific
LATEs under a multi-instrument monotonicity condition. The contribution
of the present paper is best understood as a sharp critique of how
restrictive this multi-instrument monotonicity requirement becomes.

Related work by the same authors develops complementary implications of
weakening global monotonicity. Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2024)
can be read as a direct extension of the present paper's perspective
from the interpretation of the linear 2SLS estimand to the
identification of broader policy-relevant objects. While the present
paper characterizes what 2SLS means under partial monotonicity, Mogstad,
Torgovitsky, and Walters (2024) uses the same partial monotonicity
restriction to extract and aggregate information from multiple
instruments for target parameters beyond 2SLS. In this sense, it extends
the present paper's core message, that interpretation depends on
behavioral restrictions, into a framework aimed at policy evaluation
rather than linear estimation.

Subsequent work has also taken the present paper's assumptions as
objects of empirical scrutiny. Jiang and Sun (2023) addresses a
practical implication of the present paper: once one replaces the strong
global monotonicity requirement with partial monotonicity, a natural
question is whether partial monotonicity is plausible in applications.
The paper responds by proposing a simple nonparametric test for partial
monotonicity, assessing its finite-sample behavior in simulations, and
illustrating it in an empirical application.

A related, more structural refinement is provided by Goff (2024). This
paper shares the present Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021)
motivation, in that global multi-instrument monotonicity is often too
restrictive, but proposes a different weakening, vector monotonicity,
which requires treatment uptake to be monotone in each instrument
without imposing a global ordering when instruments shift in opposing
directions. Relative to the present paper, its contribution is to
characterize which causal parameters become point identified under this
alternative restriction and to provide constructive estimation results.
Conceptually, it highlights that once global monotonicity is relaxed,
different monotonicity notions lead to different identifiable objects,
reinforcing the present paper's central emphasis on interpretability
rather than mechanical estimation.

\section{Methodology Overview}\label{methodology-overview}

Following the motivation outlined in Section~\ref{sec-intro}, this
second chapter contains the methodology developed by Mogstad,
Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021).

\subsection{Framework and Assumptions}\label{framework-and-assumptions}

\subsubsection{Setup and Potential
Outcomes}\label{setup-and-potential-outcomes}

To fix ideas and keep the exposition transparent, this section focuses
on the simplest nontrivial setting considered in the paper: a
\textbf{binary treatment} and
\textbf{two binary instrumental variables}. While the theoretical
results extend to more general environments, the central insights
already arise in this minimal framework.

Let \(D_i \in \{0,1\}\) denote a binary treatment and \(Y_i\) the
observed outcome for individual \(i\). Each individual is characterized
by potential outcomes \(Y_i(1)\) and \(Y_i(0)\), corresponding to the
potential outcomes under treatment and control, respectively. The
observed outcome can thus be expressed as: \begin{equation}
Y_i = D_i Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i) Y_i(0).
\end{equation}

Treatment assignment depends on two binary instruments, \begin{equation}
Z_i = (Z_{i1}, Z_{i2}) \in \{0,1\}^2,
\end{equation} which generate four possible instrument realizations:
\((0,0)\), \((0,1)\), \((1,0)\), and \((1,1)\). Treatment choice is
described by a potential treatment function \(D_i(z_1, z_2)\), allowing
for heterogeneous responses across individuals and differential
responses to the two instruments.

\subsubsection{2SLS Estimand}\label{sls-estimand}

The object of interest is the two-stage least squares estimand, denoted
\(\beta_{2SLS}\). The population 2SLS estimand can be written as
\begin{equation}
\beta_{2SLS} = \frac{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}_i\tilde{Y}_i]}{\mathbb{E}[\tilde{D}_i^2]},
\end{equation} where
\(\tilde{D}_i = \mathbb{E}[D_i|Z_i] - \mathbb{E}[D_i]\) and
\(\tilde{Y}_i = \mathbb{e}[Y_i|Z_i] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i]\) denote the
residualized treatment and outcome with respect to the instrument space.
Equivalently, \(\beta_{2SLS}\) can be expressed as \begin{equation}
\beta_{2SLS} = \frac{\text{Cov}(\mathbb{E}[D_i|Z_i], \mathbb{E}[Y_i|Z_i]),}{\text{Var}(\mathbb{E}[D_i|Z_i])}.
\end{equation} In the binary-instrument setting, this representation
highlights that the 2SLS estimand aggregates outcome differences across
instrument-induced changes in treatment propensity. Under standard
instrumental variables assumptions, this estimand is well defined and
consistently estimated by sample 2SLS.

The focus of the present paper is not on estimation or consistency, but
on the causal interpretation of \(\beta_{2SLS}\). In particular, the
central question is, under which assumptions on treatment choice
behavior can \(\beta_{2SLS}\) be written as a weighted average of causal
effects, and which causal effects are being aggregated? Addressing this
question requires explicit assumptions on how the instruments affect
treatment.

\subsection{Imbens and Angrist Monotonicity
(IAM)}\label{imbens-and-angrist-monotonicity-iam}

A natural starting point is the extension of the standard monotonicity
assumption from the single-instrument LATE framework, known as Imbens
and Angrist Monotonicity (IAM).

\textbf{Definition 1 (Imbens and Angrist Monotonicity)}

\textit{For all $z, z' \in \mathcal{Z}$ either $D_i(z) \ge D_i(z')$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, or $D_i(z) \le D_i(z')$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$.}

Under IAM, there must exist a global ordering of the four instrument
values such that, for every individual, treatment status moves weakly in
the same direction along this ordering.

When IAM holds, the 2SLS estimand admits a causal interpretation as a
weighted average of local average treatment effects. Importantly, IAM is
not an assumption imposed separately on each instrument. Rather, it is a
joint restriction across all instrument combinations.

While this monotonicity assumption in the single-instrument case simply
rules out defiers and is often viewed as a mild behavioral restriction,
IAM becomes substantially more demanding when multiple instruments are
present. In particular, it places strong constraints on heterogeneity in
treatment choice by requiring all instruments to operate along the same
margin of individuals.

\subsection{Partial Monotonicity}\label{partial-monotonicity}

To address the restrictiveness of IAM, the paper proposes a weaker
alternative called partial monotonicity.

\textbf{Definition 2 (Partial Monotonicity)}

\textit{Take any $l = 1, \dots, L$, and let $(z_l, z_{-l})$ and $(z'_l, z_{-l})$ be two points in $\mathcal{Z}$. Then, either $D_i(z_l, z_{-l}) \ge D_i(z'_l, z_{-l})$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$, or or $D_i(z_l, z_{-l}) \le D_i(z'_l, z_{-l})$ for all $i \in \mathcal{I}$.}

Partial monotonicity relaxes the global ordering requirement imposed by
IAM while retaining enough structure to support causal interpretation.

Formally, partial monotonicity requires that each instrument affects
treatment in a weakly monotone way when
\textbf{holding the other instrument fixed}. That is, increasing
\(Z_{i1}\) from 0 to 1 cannot induce some individuals to enter treatment
and others to exit treatment conditional on \(Z_{i2}\), and analogously
for \(Z_{i2}\).

Crucially, partial monotonicity does not impose a global ordering across
all instrument combinations. As a result, it allows exactly the type of
heterogeneity that IAM rules out. Different instruments may affect
different subpopulations, one instrument may induce treatment for one
group of individuals, while the other operates on a distinct margin.
From an economic perspective, this behavior is often natural when
instruments capture different incentives or constraints.

\subsubsection{Response Groups under Partial
Monotonicity}\label{response-groups-under-partial-monotonicity}

The partial monotonicity assumption implies that individuals can be
classified into distinct response groups based on how their treatment
status responds to changes in each instrument. In the binary-instrument
setting, we have \begin{equation}
Z_i = (Z_{i1}, Z_{i2}) \in \{0,1\}^2,
\end{equation} which generate four possible instrument values
\(z_1 = (0,0), z_2 = (0,1), z_3 = (1,0), z_4 = (1,1)\). Treatment choice
is described by the potential treatment function \(D_i(z_1,z_2)\), which
specifies whether individual \(i\) receives treatment under each
instrument realization.

The following proposition, establishes that the population can be
partitioned into six exclusive response groups.

\textbf{Proposition 1}\footnote{This proposition is adapted from
  Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021).}:

\textit{If $\mathcal{Z} = \{0,1\}^2$ and partial monotonicity holds with the following order:}
\begin{equation}
D_i(0,0) \le D_i(0,1) \le D_i(1,1),
\end{equation} \textit{and} \begin{equation}
D_i(0,0) \le D_i(1,0) \le D_i(1,1),
\end{equation}
\textit{then each $i \in \mathcal{I}$ belongs exactly to one of the six groups in Table 1.}

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{c c c c c}
\hline
\textbf{Group} $G_i$ & $D_i(0,0)$ & $D_i(0,1)$
 & $D_i(1,0)$ & $D_i(1,1)$ \\
\hline
Always-taker (\textbf{at}) & 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
Eager complier (\textbf{ec}) & 0 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\
Reluctant complier (\textbf{rc}) & 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \\
Never-taker (\textbf{nt}) & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
$Z_1$ complier (\textbf{c1}) & 0 & 0 & 1 & 1 \\
$Z_2$ complier (\textbf{c2}) & 0 & 1 & 0 & 1 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\caption{Response Groups under Partial Monotonicity}
\end{table}

Since the focus is on the causal interpretation of 2SLS, four of these
six groups are particularly relevant:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{$Z_1$ compliers (c1)}: individuals whose treatment status changes when $Z_{i1}$ switches from 0 to 1, holding $Z_{i2}$ fixed, but who do not respond to changes in $Z_{i2}$.
\item \textbf{$Z_2$ compliers (c2)}: individuals whose treatment status changes when $Z_{i2}$ switches from 0 to 1, holding $Z_{i1}$ fixed, but who do not respond to changes in $Z_{i1}$.
\item \textbf{Eager compliers (ec)}: individuals who enter treatment when either instrument is activated. For these individuals, both instruments independently push them into treatment.
\item \textbf{Reluctant compliers (rc)}: individuals who enter treatment only when both instruments are active. Each instrument alone is insufficient, but their joint activation induces treatment.
\end{enumerate}

These groups generalize the familiar complier concept from the
single-instrument LATE framework and reflect the fact that different
instruments may operate on different margins of the population. In
particular, individuals are allowed to respond differently to different
instruments, as long as each instrument moves treatment in a consistent
direction holding the other fixed.

\subsection{2SLS Estimand under Partial
Monotonicity}\label{sls-estimand-under-partial-monotonicity}

\subsubsection{Instrumental Variable
Assumptions}\label{instrumental-variable-assumptions}

The 2SLS estimand under partial monotonicity is derived naturally under
partial monotonicity, but also under the standard instrumental-variables
assumptions.

\textbf{Instrument Relevance:}
\textit{Each instrument has a nonzero effect on the probability of treatment:}
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 | Z_{i,l} = 1) \ne \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 | Z_{i,l} = 0), \quad l \in \{1,2\}.
\end{equation} \textbf{Instrument Exogeneity:}
\textit{The instruments are independent of potential outcomes and potential treatments:}
\begin{equation}
(Y_i(0), Y_i(1), \{D_i(z)\}_{z \in \mathcal{Z}}) \perp Z_i.
\end{equation} \textbf{Exclusion:}
\textit{Instruments affect the outcome only through treatment:}
\begin{equation}
Y_i(z) = Y_i(D_i(z)) \quad \textit{for all } z \in \mathcal{Z}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Causal Interpretation of 2SLS under Partial
Monotonicity}\label{causal-interpretation-of-2sls-under-partial-monotonicity}

\textbf{Theorem 1}\footnote{This theorem is adapted from Mogstad,
  Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021).}:

\textit{Suppose the instrument vector $Z_i = (Z_{i1}, Z_{i2})$ has support $\mathcal{Z} = \{0,1\}^2$, and that \textbf{partial monotonicity, instrument relevance, instrument exogeneity, and the exclusion restriction} hold. Suppose further that the potential outcomes are well-defined and that the 2SLS estimand $\beta_{2SLS}$ exists. Then, the 2SLS estimand can be expressed as:}
\begin{equation}
\beta_{2SLS} = \sum_{g \in \{1c, 2c, ec, rc\}} w_g \Delta_g,
\end{equation}
\textit{where $\Delta_g$ denotes the average treatment effect for response group $g$, and $w_g$ are weights with $\sum_g w_g = 1$. Both the weights of eager compliers and reluctant compliers are always nonnegative, i.e. $w_{ec}, w_{rc} \ge 0$, while one of the weights of $Z_1$ compliers or $Z_2$ compliers can be negative.}

\textit{If $\pi_{1c} \ge \pi_{2c}$ where $\pi_g := \mathbb{P}(G_i = g)$, then $w_{1c}$ is also nonnegative, while the sign of $w_{2c}$ is given by:}
\begin{equation}
\text{sgn}(w_{2c}) = \mathbb{I}[\pi_{2c} > 0] \times \text{sgn}[\mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 | Z_{i,2} = 1] - \mathbb{P}[D_i = 1 | Z_{i,2} = 0)].
\end{equation}
\textit{If $\pi_{2c} \ge \pi_{1c}$, then $w_{2c}$ is nonnegative, while the sign of $w_{1c}$ is given by}
\begin{equation}
\text{sgn}(w_{1c}) = \mathbb{I}[\pi_{1c} > 0] \times \text{sgn}[\mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 | Z_{i,1} = 1) - \mathbb{P}(D_i = 1 | Z_{i,1} = 0)].
\end{equation}

This theorem establishes that the 2SLS estimand can be interpreted as a
weighted average of average treatment effects for the four possible
groups that change treatment status in response to the one or both of
the instruments.

The economic intuition is that some changes in the instrument values,
specifically the shift from \((0,1)\) to \((1,0)\) can generate two-way
treatment flows, this means that one group of individuals enters
treatment while another exits. When this happens, the associated
complier group receives a negative weight.

This result provides a complete characterization of what 2SLS identifies
under partial monotonicity. Rather than a single LATE, the estimand
aggregates causal effects across multiple response groups, with the sign
and magnitude of the weights determined by observable first-stage
relationships.

\textbf{Proof of Theorem 1}\footnote{This proof is also adapted from
  Mogstad, Torgovitsky, and Walters (2021).}:

The goal of the proof is to show that, under partial monotonicity and
standard IV assumptions, the 2SLS estimand can be expressed as a
weighted average of group-specific treatment effects, and to
characterize precisely when one of these weights may be negative.

The proof begins by labeling the four possible realizations of the two
binary instruments as \begin{equation}
z^1 = (0,0), z^2 = (0,1), z^3 = (1,0), z^4 = (1,1),
\end{equation} and denoting their probabilites as
\(q_k = \mathbb{P}(Z_i = z^k)\) for \(k = 1, \dots, 4\). Let
\begin{equation}
p_k = \mathbb{P}[D_i = 1 | Z_i = z^k],
\end{equation} denote the propensity score associated with instrument
value \(z^k\). Under partial monotonicity and the ordering assumed in
the paper, these propensity scores are weakly increasing in \(k\). A key
result used in the proof is a result due to Angrist and Imbens, which
shows that in this setting the 2SLS estimand can be written as a convex
combination of three Wald estimands corresponding to adjacent changes in
the instrument values, i.e. \begin{equation}
\beta_{2SLS} = \lambda_2 w_2 + \lambda_3 w_3 + \lambda_4 w_4,
\end{equation} where the coefficients \(\lambda_k\) are positive weights
and sum to one. The Wald estimands \(w_k\) are defined as
\begin{equation}
w_k := \frac{\mathbb{E}[Y_i | Z_i = z^k] - \mathbb{E}[Y_i | Z_i = z^{k-1}]}{p(z^k) - p(z^{k-1})}, \quad k = 2,3,4.
\end{equation} Each Wald estimand captures the average causal effect for
individuals whose treatment status changes when the instrument moves
from \(z^{k-1}\) to \(z^k\). The weights \(\lambda_k\) are defined as
\begin{equation}
\lambda_k := \frac{(p(z^k) - p(z^{k-1}))\sum_{l=k}^4 q^l(p(z^l) - \mathbb{E}[p(Z_i)])}{\sum_{j=2}^4 [(p(z^j) - p(z^{j-1})) \sum_{l=j}^4 q^l (p(z^l) - \mathbb{E}[p(Z_i)])]}.
\end{equation} The group definitions from Proposition 1 implies that
\(w_2\) represents the average treatment effect for both the \(Z_2\)
compliers and the eager compliers. Similarly, \(w_4\) captures the
average treatment effect for \(Z_2\) compliers and reluctant compliers,
i.e. \begin{equation}
w_2 = \left(\frac{\pi_{2c}}{\pi_{2c} + \pi_{ec}}\right)\Delta_{2c} + \left(\frac{\pi_{ec}}{\pi_{2c} + \pi_{ec}}\right)\Delta_{ec}
\end{equation} and \begin{equation}
w_4 = \left(\frac{\pi_{2c}}{\pi_{2c} + \pi_{rc}}\right)\Delta_{2c} + \left(\frac{\pi_{rc}}{\pi_{2c} + \pi_{rc}}\right)\Delta_{rc}.
\end{equation} For the transitions from \(z^1 \rightarrow z^2\) and from
\(z^3 \rightarrow z^4\), only one instrument changes while the other is
held fixed. Under partial monotonicity, these transitions induce
one-directional treatment movements, i.e.~individuals either enter
treatment or remain untreated. As a result, the corresponding Wald
estimands can be written as weighted averages of treatment effects for
well-defined complier groups. Importantly, all weights involved in these
decompositions are nonnegative.

However, the crucial part comes from the transition from
\(z^2 = (0,1) \rightarrow z^3 = (1,0)\), which is different. Along this
margin, one instrument increases while the other decreases. Partial
monotonicity allows this change to generate
\textbf{two-way treatment flows}, where \(Z_1\) compliers enter
treatment while \(Z_2\) compliers exit treatment. Consequently, the Wald
estimand \(w_3\) can be expressed as \begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
w_3 &= \frac{\mathbb{E}[(Y_i(1) - Y_i(0))(D_i(1,0) - D_i(0,1))]}{p(z^3) - p(z^2)}\\ &= \left(\frac{\pi_{1c}}{p(z^3) - p(z^2)}\right)\Delta_{1c} + \left(\frac{\pi_{2c}}{p(z^3) - p(z^2)} \right)\Delta_{2c} \\ &= \left(\frac{\pi_{1c}}{\pi_{1c} - \pi_{2c}}\right)\Delta_{1c} + \left(\frac{\pi_{2c}}{\pi_{1c} - \pi_{2c}} \right)\Delta_{2c}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation} Substituting the decompositions of the three Wald
estimands back into the convex combination for \(\beta_{2SLS}\) yields
\begin{equation}
\beta_{2SLS} = \sum_{g \in \{1c, 2c, ec, rc\}} w_g \Delta_g,
\end{equation} where \[
\begin{aligned}
w_{ec} &:= \frac{\lambda_{2}\pi_{ec}}{\pi_{ec} + \pi_{2c}}, \\
w_{1c} &:= \frac{\lambda_3 \pi_{1c}}{\pi_{1c} - \pi_{2c}}, \\
w_{rc} &:= \frac{\lambda_4 \pi_{rc}}{\pi_{rc} + \pi_{2c}}, \\
w_{2c} &:= 
\left(\frac{\lambda_{2}\pi_{2c}}{\pi_{ec} + \pi_{2c}}\right)
- \left(\frac{\lambda_3\pi_{2c}}{\pi_{1c} - \pi_{2c}}\right)
+ \left(\frac{\lambda_4 \pi_{2c}}{\pi_{rc} + \pi_{2c}}\right).
\end{aligned}
\] The proof shows that the weights on eager and reluctant compliers are
always nonnegative, and that at most one instrument-specific complier
group can receive a negative weight. Moreover, the sign of this
potentially negative weight is not arbitrary, it is entirely determined
by an observable first-stage object, specifically by the covariance
between treatment and the corresponding instrument. In particular, the
weight on \(Z_2\) compliers is negative if and only if the first-stage
effect of \(Z_2\) on treatment is negative along the relevant margin.

The central insight of the proof is therefore that negative weights
arise only when an instrument change induces offsetting treatment flows,
i.e.~some individuals enter treatment while others exit. Apart from this
specific case, all components of the 2SLS estimand receive nonnegative
weight.
